1. Problem Statement

Electric arc furnaces (EAFs) consume substantial electrical energy to melt scrap metal. Active power is highly dynamic, driven by arc ignition, electrode movement, and varying furnace conditions.

Why predict active power?

  • Energy cost optimization — electricity constitutes 60–70% of EAF operating costs
  • Grid stability — EAFs are among the largest and most disruptive industrial loads, causing voltage flicker and harmonic distortion
  • Process control — real-time forecasts inform adaptive electrode positioning and power setpoint strategies
Objective: Predict total active power (MW) at 1-minute resolution using a 60-minute lookback window of multivariate sensor data. Compare LSTM vs. Transformer architectures.

7. LSTM — Architecture & Training

How LSTM works

Long Short-Term Memory cells use three gates to regulate information flow through a recurrent cell state:

  • Forget gate — discards irrelevant past information
  • Input gate — writes new information to cell state
  • Output gate — reads from filtered cell state

This gating mechanism solves the vanishing gradient problem, enabling learning of long-range temporal dependencies (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997).

8. Transformer — Architecture & Training

How Transformers work

Transformers replace recurrence with multi-head self-attention, allowing each position to attend to all others in constant path length (Vaswani et al., 2017).

  • Self-attention — computes weighted relationships across all timesteps in parallel
  • Positional encoding — sinusoidal signals inject order information (no inherent sequence notion)
  • Feedforward layers — per-position nonlinear transformations

11. Discussion

Overall performance

Both models achieve R² ≈ 0.90 and RMSE < 2 MW. With a target mean of 27.33 MW, this represents a normalized error (RMSE/mean) of ~7% — operationally useful for short-term power management.

Why does LSTM outperform the Transformer here?

1. Dataset size
~40K training sequences is modest for Transformers, which typically need more data to realize their expressiveness (Zeng et al., 2023).
2. Sequential inductive bias
LSTMs naturally weight recent observations via the forget gate. For 1-minute EAF data with strong local autocorrelation, this bias is advantageous.
3. Parameter efficiency
LSTM: 52,801 params, 56s training.
Transformer: 67,713 params (+28%), 93s training (+66%).
Comparable results with fewer resources.
Practical recommendation
For real-time EAF control: prefer LSTM (lower RMSE, faster inference, simpler deployment). Transformer's lower MAE may suit average-case optimization scenarios.

12. Conclusions & Future Work

Key findings

  1. Both models are effective — R² ≥ 0.887, RMSE < 2 MW for 1-minute-ahead forecasting of EAF active power.
  2. LSTM holds a slight edge — outperforms on 3/4 metrics with 22% fewer parameters and 40% less training time.
  3. Transformer shows promise — lower MAE and continued improvement in later epochs suggest it may benefit from more data or longer training.
  4. Feature selection matters — reducing 43 columns to 10 physically meaningful features achieved strong performance without overfitting.

Future directions

  • Multi-step forecasting — predict 5, 15, 30 min ahead using seq2seq architectures
  • Attention interpretability — visualize which timesteps and features drive predictions
  • Hybrid architectures — CNN-LSTM or CNN-Transformer for local + global patterns
  • Efficient Transformers — Informer, Autoformer designed for long sequences
  • Online learning — adapt to changing conditions (electrode wear, scrap variability)
  • Expanded dataset — multi-month data for generalization and robustness
